# The menstrual lifespan

*From first period through menopause — and why postmenopausal bleeding always needs checking.*

## Puberty and menarche

The first period usually arrives between **10 and 15** (average around **12**), typically about **2 to 2.5 years** after breast development begins and following a growth spurt and the appearance of vaginal discharge. Genetics, nutrition, body composition, and general health all influence timing.

**Cycles are commonly irregular for the first 1–2 years** because ovulation isn't yet consistent — cycles up to **45 days** are normal in that window. Practical preparation matters more than precision here: a small kit in a school bag, knowing where to get supplies, and knowing that the first period arriving unexpectedly is completely ordinary.

Worth medical attention: no period by **15**, no signs of puberty by **13**, or periods that are extremely heavy or disabling from the start.

## Reproductive years

Cycles typically settle into their most regular pattern in the 20s and 30s. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, contraception, weight change, and stress will all interrupt them, and cycles usually resume afterwards.

Fertility declines gradually from the early 30s and more steeply after **35** — but regular periods continuing doesn't guarantee fertility, and irregular ones don't rule it out.

## Perimenopause

The transition, often starting in the **mid-40s** (sometimes earlier) and lasting anywhere from a few months to over **10 years**. Oestrogen fluctuates erratically rather than simply declining, which is why symptoms can be so variable.

Signs: cycles shortening then lengthening, skipped periods, changes in flow (often heavier before becoming lighter), hot flushes and night sweats, sleep disruption, mood changes, brain fog, vaginal dryness, joint aches, and worsening PMS.

**Pregnancy is still possible during perimenopause**, so contraception remains relevant — generally until **12 months** after the last period if over **50**, or **24 months** if under **50**.

## Menopause

Defined retrospectively as **12 consecutive months with no period**. Average age is around **51** (roughly **45–55** is typical). Before **45** is early; before **40** is premature ovarian insufficiency.

**Any bleeding after menopause is not normal and needs prompt medical assessment** — it's usually a benign cause, but it's also the main early symptom of endometrial cancer, which is highly treatable when caught early. This is the single most important line in this section.

## Postmenopause

Symptoms often ease over time, though vaginal and urinary changes tend to persist and are very treatable with local oestrogen. Long-term, lower oestrogen means increased risk of osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease — which makes strength training, adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D, and blood pressure monitoring genuinely worth attending to. HRT is an option worth an informed discussion with a doctor; the risk picture is considerably more favourable than the widely-publicised early-2000s reporting suggested, particularly when started near the time of menopause.

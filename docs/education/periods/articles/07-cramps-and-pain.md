# Cramps and pain

*Why periods hurt, when pain is a warning, and what actually helps.*

## Why it hurts

The endometrium releases **prostaglandins**, hormone-like compounds that make the myometrium contract to expel the lining. Those contractions also temporarily squeeze blood vessels, briefly reducing oxygen to the uterine muscle — which is genuinely painful, mechanically similar to why angina hurts.

Prostaglandins circulate beyond the uterus, which is why period cramps often come with **nausea, diarrhoea, headache, and lower back or thigh pain**. People with higher prostaglandin levels have worse cramps.

## Primary vs secondary dysmenorrhoea

**Primary** — pain from the normal process itself, with no underlying disease. Typically starts within a year or two of menarche, begins just before or with bleeding, lasts 1–3 days, and often eases with age or after childbirth.

**Secondary** — pain caused by an underlying condition such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, or fibroids. Clues that pain is secondary: it started years after periods began, it's getting worse over time, it lasts longer than the bleeding, it doesn't respond to NSAIDs, or it comes with pain during sex or bowel movements.

## What helps

- **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, mefenamic acid)** — the most effective over-the-counter option, because they block prostaglandin *production* rather than just masking pain. **Take them at the first sign of the period, before pain peaks** — this is the single biggest lever, and most people take them too late. Take with food; not suitable for everyone (asthma, stomach ulcers, kidney issues, certain medications).
- **Heat** — a heating pad or hot water bottle on the lower abdomen performs comparably to NSAIDs in some trials, and stacks with them.
- **Hormonal contraception** — thins the endometrium so there's less to shed and fewer prostaglandins. Often dramatically effective.
- **Exercise** — regular aerobic activity reduces pain over time, even though moving during a bad cramp feels unappealing.
- **Sleep, hydration, and reduced alcohol** — modest but real effects.
- **TENS machines, magnesium** — evidence is mixed but some people find them useful.

Paracetamol/acetaminophen works less well for cramps than NSAIDs, because it doesn't act on prostaglandins in the same way.

# When to see a doctor

*Practical thresholds — and how to make the appointment count.*

## Book an appointment if you have

- Bleeding through a pad or tampon every hour for several consecutive hours
- Periods lasting longer than **7 days**
- Clots consistently bigger than a **10p coin / quarter (~2.5 cm)**
- Pain that stops you attending work or school, or that painkillers don't touch
- Cycles consistently shorter than **21 days** or longer than **35**
- No period for **3+ months** (with pregnancy ruled out)
- Bleeding between periods, after sex, or after menopause
- Symptoms of anaemia: persistent fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness, pale skin
- Severe premenstrual mood symptoms affecting your relationships or work
- Pain during sex, urination, or bowel movements
- A significant, sustained change from your own normal
- No period by age **15**, or no signs of puberty by **13**
- Trouble conceiving after **12 months** of trying (or **6 months** if over **35**)

## Seek urgent care for

- Sudden severe pelvic pain, especially with fever, fainting, or vomiting
- Signs of toxic shock syndrome while using a tampon or cup: high fever, vomiting, rash, dizziness, confusion
- Very heavy bleeding with dizziness, a racing heart, or breathlessness
- A positive pregnancy test with severe one-sided pain and/or bleeding (possible ectopic pregnancy — a medical emergency)

## Making the appointment count

Bring **2–3 cycles** of tracked data — dates, flow, pain scores, symptoms. Be specific about *function* rather than describing pain in the abstract: "I miss one day of work every month" lands very differently from "my periods are painful." And if you feel dismissed, it is entirely reasonable to ask for a second opinion or a specialist referral. Delayed diagnosis in menstrual health is well documented, and self-advocacy is a legitimate and often necessary part of the process.

# Periods: A Complete Guide

*A plain-language reference to the menstrual cycle — how it works, what's normal, what isn't, and what to do about it.*

> This guide is general information, not medical advice. Bodies vary, and anything persistent, severe, or newly different for you deserves a conversation with a doctor or nurse who can examine you and knows your history.

Most people who menstruate are women, and much of the literature uses that language. Trans men, non-binary people, and people with intersex variations may also menstruate, and some women don't. The biology in this guide applies to anyone with a uterus and ovaries; use whichever framing fits.

# 1. The short version


*What a period is, and how it sits inside the wider cycle.*

A period is the shedding of the lining of the uterus. It happens when an egg released that month isn't fertilised, hormone levels drop, and the lining — which had been thickening in preparation for a pregnancy — breaks down and leaves the body through the vagina.

The period is just one visible part of a larger, roughly monthly hormonal cycle. That cycle typically runs **21 to 35 days** in adults, with bleeding lasting **2 to 7 days**. Most people lose only **30–80 ml** of blood in total — a few tablespoons — though it usually looks like far more.

Cycles begin at puberty (**menarche**, usually ages 10–15) and end at **menopause** (average around 51). In between, they can be knocked off course by stress, illness, weight change, exercise, travel, contraception, pregnancy, and breastfeeding.

# 2. The anatomy involved


*The organs — and the brain circuit — that make a cycle possible.*

## Ovaries

Two almond-sized organs holding all the eggs a person will ever have (roughly **1–2 million** at birth, declining to around **300,000–400,000** by puberty, of which only about **400** are ever ovulated). They also produce oestrogen and progesterone.

## Fallopian tubes

Two narrow tubes carrying the released egg toward the uterus. Fertilisation, when it happens, usually occurs here rather than in the uterus.

## Uterus (womb)

A muscular, pear-sized organ. Its inner lining, the **endometrium**, is what thickens and sheds each cycle. Its muscular wall, the **myometrium**, does the contracting that causes cramps.

## Cervix

The narrow neck at the base of the uterus opening into the vagina. Its mucus changes texture across the cycle, which is why cervical mucus is useful for tracking fertility.

## Vagina

The muscular canal through which menstrual fluid exits.

## Hypothalamus and pituitary gland

In the brain, not the pelvis — but they're the control room. They release the signalling hormones that tell the ovaries what to do, and they respond to feedback from the ovaries in turn. This brain–ovary loop is called the **HPO axis** (hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis), and it's why stress, illness, undereating, and overtraining can stop periods: the brain is part of the circuit.

# 3. The hormones that run the cycle


*Rise and fall matter more than absolute levels — and four hormones do most of the work.*

## GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone)

Released in pulses by the hypothalamus. Sets the tempo for everything downstream.

## FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone)

From the pituitary. Recruits a cohort of follicles (fluid-filled sacs, each holding an immature egg) in the ovaries and stimulates them to mature.

## LH (luteinizing hormone)

Also from the pituitary. Rises gradually, then spikes sharply about **24–36 hours** before ovulation. That **LH surge** is the trigger that releases the egg, and it's what home ovulation predictor kits detect in urine.

## Oestrogen (mainly oestradiol)

Produced by the growing follicle. Rebuilds and thickens the endometrium, thins cervical mucus so sperm can travel, and peaks just before ovulation. Many people report their best energy, mood, and sleep during the high-oestrogen stretch.

## Progesterone

Produced after ovulation by the **corpus luteum**, the structure the emptied follicle becomes. It stabilises the endometrium, thickens cervical mucus again, and raises basal body temperature slightly. It also has a sedating, sometimes low-mood effect for many people. Its steep drop at the end of the cycle is what actually triggers the period.

## Testosterone

Present in smaller amounts, peaking around ovulation. Contributes to libido.


A useful mental model: **oestrogen builds, progesterone holds, and the withdrawal of progesterone releases.**

# 4. The cycle, phase by phase


*Day 1 is the first day of proper bleeding — not spotting the day before.*

## Menstrual phase (days 1–5, roughly)

Progesterone and oestrogen are at their lowest. The endometrium loses its blood supply, breaks down, and is expelled by uterine contractions. Flow is usually heaviest on days 1–2 and tapers after.

Low hormones mean many people feel tired and low-energy in the first day or two, then noticeably better as oestrogen begins climbing again around days 3–4.

## Follicular phase (day 1 until ovulation)

Overlaps with the period, then continues after it. FSH matures a group of follicles; within about a week one becomes **dominant** and the rest degenerate. The dominant follicle pumps out rising oestrogen, which rebuilds the endometrium from a thin layer to several millimetres thick.

**This is the variable phase.** If someone's cycles are 24 days one month and 33 the next, it's almost always the follicular phase stretching or shortening. Stress, illness, and travel delay ovulation and therefore lengthen this phase.

## Ovulation (a single event, mid-cycle)

Rising oestrogen crosses a threshold and flips the pituitary from suppression into a burst — the LH surge. Roughly **24–36 hours** later the dominant follicle ruptures and releases its egg into the fallopian tube.

Signs, if you look for them:

- **Cervical mucus** becomes clear, slippery, and stretchy — commonly described as raw egg white
- **Mittelschmerz** — a one-sided pelvic twinge or ache lasting hours to a day, felt by around 1 in 5 people
- **Basal body temperature** rises about **0.3–0.5 °C** *after* ovulation and stays up (confirms it happened; doesn't predict it)
- Higher libido, and for some, slight spotting or breast tenderness

### Fertility window

The egg survives only **12–24 hours**. Sperm can survive up to **5 days** in fertile cervical mucus. So the fertile window is roughly the **5 days before ovulation plus the day of it — about 6 days total**, with the two days before ovulation being the most fertile.

## Luteal phase (ovulation to the next period)

The collapsed follicle becomes the corpus luteum and secretes progesterone, which makes the endometrium receptive to an embryo.

The luteal phase is remarkably **consistent at 12–14 days** in most people, regardless of total cycle length. This is the basis of a useful rule: if you know when your period started, you can estimate ovulation as roughly **14 days before the next period**, not 14 days after the last one.

If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum degenerates after about **10–12 days**. Progesterone and oestrogen fall sharply. The endometrium loses support, and the next period begins. If an embryo *does* implant, it produces **hCG** — the hormone pregnancy tests detect — which keeps the corpus luteum alive and progesterone high, so no period occurs.

Most PMS symptoms cluster in the last several days of this phase, as hormones fall.

# 5. What menstrual blood actually is


*It isn't purely blood — and that explains a lot of what people find alarming.*

Menstrual fluid is roughly half blood, plus endometrial tissue, cervical mucus, and vaginal secretions.

- **Bright red** — fresh, fast flow, typical of the heaviest days
- **Dark red, brown, or nearly black** — older blood that oxidised on its way out; very common at the start and end of a period
- **Pink or watery** — light flow diluted with cervical fluid
- **Small clots** — normal, especially on heavy days. The body releases anticoagulants to keep flow liquid, but on a heavy day it can't keep up. **Clots consistently larger than a 10p coin / a quarter (~2.5 cm) are worth mentioning to a doctor.**
- **Grey or foul-smelling discharge** — not normal; see a doctor, as it can indicate infection

Total loss averages **30–80 ml** across the whole period. Over **80 ml** is clinically classed as heavy (menorrhagia) — but nobody measures this at home, so practical markers are used instead. See [When to see a doctor](15-when-to-see-a-doctor.md).

# 6. What counts as normal


*"Normal" is a range, not a number — and your own baseline matters most.*

| Measure | Typical range | Worth investigating |
|---|---|---|
| Cycle length (day 1 to day 1) | 21–35 days (adults); up to 45 in the first years | Consistently <21 or >35 days |
| Cycle-to-cycle variation | Up to ~7–9 days | Wildly erratic, or a sudden change from your own baseline |
| Bleeding duration | 2–7 days | >7 days |
| Total blood loss | 30–80 ml | Soaking a pad/tampon hourly for several hours; large clots |
| Pain | Manageable, responds to NSAIDs | Pain that stops you working, studying, or sleeping |
| PMS | Mild-to-moderate, resolves once bleeding starts | Symptoms that damage relationships or function |

Two things worth internalising:

**"Normal" is a range, not a number.** A 24-day cycle and a 34-day cycle are both fine. The 28-day figure is an average, not a standard.

**Your own baseline matters more than the population average.** A meaningful, sustained change from what's normal *for you* is a better reason to check in with a doctor than falling slightly outside a textbook range.

# 7. Cramps and pain


*Why periods hurt, when pain is a warning, and what actually helps.*

## Why it hurts

The endometrium releases **prostaglandins**, hormone-like compounds that make the myometrium contract to expel the lining. Those contractions also temporarily squeeze blood vessels, briefly reducing oxygen to the uterine muscle — which is genuinely painful, mechanically similar to why angina hurts.

Prostaglandins circulate beyond the uterus, which is why period cramps often come with **nausea, diarrhoea, headache, and lower back or thigh pain**. People with higher prostaglandin levels have worse cramps.

## Primary vs secondary dysmenorrhoea

**Primary** — pain from the normal process itself, with no underlying disease. Typically starts within a year or two of menarche, begins just before or with bleeding, lasts 1–3 days, and often eases with age or after childbirth.

**Secondary** — pain caused by an underlying condition such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, or fibroids. Clues that pain is secondary: it started years after periods began, it's getting worse over time, it lasts longer than the bleeding, it doesn't respond to NSAIDs, or it comes with pain during sex or bowel movements.

## What helps

- **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, mefenamic acid)** — the most effective over-the-counter option, because they block prostaglandin *production* rather than just masking pain. **Take them at the first sign of the period, before pain peaks** — this is the single biggest lever, and most people take them too late. Take with food; not suitable for everyone (asthma, stomach ulcers, kidney issues, certain medications).
- **Heat** — a heating pad or hot water bottle on the lower abdomen performs comparably to NSAIDs in some trials, and stacks with them.
- **Hormonal contraception** — thins the endometrium so there's less to shed and fewer prostaglandins. Often dramatically effective.
- **Exercise** — regular aerobic activity reduces pain over time, even though moving during a bad cramp feels unappealing.
- **Sleep, hydration, and reduced alcohol** — modest but real effects.
- **TENS machines, magnesium** — evidence is mixed but some people find them useful.

Paracetamol/acetaminophen works less well for cramps than NSAIDs, because it doesn't act on prostaglandins in the same way.

# 8. PMS and PMDD


*Common luteal-phase symptoms — and a severe mood disorder that needs care, not endurance.*

## PMS (premenstrual syndrome)

Symptoms appearing in the luteal phase and resolving within a few days of bleeding starting. Around **3 in 4** menstruating people experience some version of it.

**Physical:** bloating, breast tenderness, fluid retention, headaches, acne, joint or muscle aches, food cravings, fatigue, disturbed sleep.

**Emotional/cognitive:** irritability, low mood, anxiety, tearfulness, reduced concentration, feeling overwhelmed, social withdrawal.

The mechanism isn't abnormal hormone levels — most people with PMS have entirely normal hormone profiles. It's **sensitivity to the change**, specifically to progesterone's breakdown product allopregnanolone and its effect on GABA receptors in the brain, plus serotonin fluctuations.

Helpful: consistent sleep, regular exercise, reduced alcohol and caffeine, adequate calcium and vitamin D, stress management. Hormonal contraception helps some people and worsens it for others.

## PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder)

A severe, distinct condition recognised in the DSM-5, affecting roughly **2–5%** of menstruating people by strict prospectively-confirmed criteria (studies using looser retrospective criteria report up to **8%**). The defining feature is severity of *mood* symptoms: marked depression, hopelessness, intense irritability or anger, anxiety or tension, and mood swings, severe enough to disrupt work, study, or relationships.

Diagnosis requires **prospective symptom tracking across at least two cycles** — showing symptoms confined to the luteal phase and clearing after bleeding starts. This distinguishes PMDD from depression or anxiety that a period merely worsens.

It's treatable. Options include SSRIs (which for PMDD often work within days and can be taken luteal-phase-only rather than continuously), certain hormonal contraceptives, and in severe refractory cases GnRH agonists. **PMDD is not something to endure quietly** — it carries a genuinely elevated risk of self-harm and suicidal ideation, and getting help matters.

If your premenstrual mood symptoms are severe, keeping a simple daily rating log for two cycles before seeing a doctor will substantially speed up getting the right diagnosis.

# 9. Other symptoms across the cycle


*Gut, head, skin, sleep, and conditions that ride the same hormonal wave.*

## Digestive changes

Prostaglandins affect the bowel too, so looser stools or diarrhoea around day 1 are common — as is constipation in the luteal phase, when progesterone slows gut motility. People with IBS often find symptoms track the cycle.

## Migraine

Menstrual migraine is triggered by the drop in oestrogen just before the period. It's typically more severe and less responsive to usual treatment than other migraines. If you get migraine *with aura*, that's important information for contraception choice — combined oestrogen pills are generally avoided due to stroke risk.

## Skin

Acne often flares in the late luteal phase as oestrogen falls and androgens are relatively higher.

## Breasts

Tenderness and fullness (cyclical mastalgia) is common from ovulation onward and resolves with bleeding.

## Sleep and temperature

Progesterone raises core body temperature in the luteal phase, which for some people disrupts sleep.

## Cognition and mood

Effects are real but individually variable and often overstated. Many people report sharper verbal fluency mid-cycle and lower stress tolerance premenstrually.

## Existing conditions

Asthma, epilepsy (**catamenial epilepsy**), depression, and autoimmune conditions can all fluctuate with the cycle. If yours does, tracking it alongside your cycle gives your doctor something concrete to work with.

# 10. Period products


*No medically superior option — preference, budget, and circumstance.*

## Pads (sanitary towels)

External, no insertion, easy to start with. Disposable or washable. Change every 3–4 hours on heavier days.

## Tampons

Internal, absorbent, invisible under clothing. **Change every 4–8 hours and never exceed 8**, and use the lowest absorbency that handles your flow. This is because of **toxic shock syndrome (TSS)** — a rare (roughly **1–3 cases per 100,000** menstruating people per year) but rapidly dangerous bacterial toxin illness. Warning signs: sudden high fever, vomiting, a sunburn-like rash, dizziness, confusion. Remove the tampon and seek emergency care immediately if these appear. Rarity is not a reason to be casual about timing.

## Menstrual cups

Reusable silicone or rubber cups that collect rather than absorb. Hold 2–3 times a tampon's volume, can be worn up to 12 hours, last years, and are far cheaper and lower-waste over time. There's a real learning curve of a cycle or two. Wash between uses and sterilise in boiling water between cycles.

## Menstrual discs

Similar idea, sit higher, in the vaginal fornix rather than the canal. Some can be worn during penetrative sex.

## Period underwear

Absorbent layers built into normal-looking underwear. Good alone on light days, good as backup on heavy days.

## Free bleeding / reusable cloth pads

Both viable choices with the obvious practical trade-offs.

## Period poverty

The cost of products is a real barrier for many people, and a growing number of countries and regions now provide them free in schools, universities, and public buildings. Worth knowing if you or someone you know needs them.

# 11. Tracking your cycle


*Turns a vague experience into data you can act on — and into something a doctor can actually use.*

## What to record

**Minimum useful record:** the date each period starts, how many days it lasts, and a rough flow rating.

**More detailed:** pain scores, mood ratings, symptoms, sleep, cervical mucus, basal body temperature.

## Methods

- **Calendar/app.** Convenient. Prediction accuracy is only as good as your cycle's regularity — apps assume ovulation on a schedule and are frequently wrong for irregular cycles.
- **Basal body temperature (BBT).** Temperature taken immediately on waking, before moving, each day. Confirms ovulation happened (a sustained rise) but only in hindsight.
- **Cervical mucus (Billings method).** Observing the change to clear, stretchy mucus. Predicts ovulation prospectively, unlike BBT.
- **LH strips.** Detect the surge **24–36 hours** before ovulation. The most precise consumer prediction method.
- **Symptothermal method.** Combining mucus and BBT. When taught properly and followed strictly, it's the most reliable of the fertility-awareness approaches — but "typical use" failure rates for contraception are considerably worse than "perfect use," and it demands real discipline.

## Privacy

Period-tracking apps hold sensitive health data, and data-sharing and law-enforcement-access practices vary by app and jurisdiction. If that matters to you, check the privacy policy, prefer apps that store data locally on your device, or use a paper diary or a plain notes file.

TrackHer is built for personal record-keeping. Whatever you use, **2–3 cycles of dates, flow, pain, and symptoms** make a clinic visit substantially more useful — see [When to see a doctor](15-when-to-see-a-doctor.md).

# 12. Hormonal birth control and the cycle


*Worth understanding because it changes the whole picture.*

## Combined pill (oestrogen + progestin)

Suppresses ovulation. The bleed during the placebo/break week isn't a real period — it's **withdrawal bleeding** caused by stopping the hormones, and it's typically lighter and less painful. It exists largely for historical and reassurance reasons, not medical necessity; skipping the break to avoid bleeding is generally considered safe, but discuss it with your prescriber.

## Progestin-only pill (mini-pill)

May or may not suppress ovulation depending on type. Bleeding patterns are often unpredictable.

## Hormonal IUD (e.g. Mirena, Kyleena)

Thins the endometrium locally. Often dramatically reduces bleeding and cramps; many users' periods become very light or stop entirely, which is safe. Frequently prescribed specifically to treat heavy bleeding.

## Copper IUD

Non-hormonal, so ovulation continues normally — but it commonly makes periods **heavier and crampier**, especially in the first few months.

## Implant, injection, ring, patch

Each with its own bleeding profile; irregular spotting is common early on with the implant and injection.

## Beyond contraception

Hormonal methods are legitimately used to treat heavy bleeding, severe cramps, endometriosis, PCOS symptoms, PMDD, acne, and menstrual migraine.

## Coming off it

Cycles may take a few months to re-establish their own rhythm. Whatever your periods were like before starting, they'll typically return to roughly that — the contraception was managing symptoms, not curing the underlying cause.

# 13. Conditions and disorders


## Endometriosis

Tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus — on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, bowel, bladder, or pelvic wall. It responds to cycle hormones and bleeds, but has nowhere to go, causing inflammation, scarring, and adhesions.

Affects roughly **1 in 10** women and people with a uterus of reproductive age. Diagnosis has historically taken an average of **7–10 years** (studies range from about **1.5 to 11 years** depending on country), largely because severe period pain gets normalised — by patients and clinicians alike.

## Symptoms

Severe pelvic pain (often worse than "bad cramps" and often starting before the period), pain during or after sex, painful bowel movements or urination especially during periods, heavy bleeding, chronic fatigue, and difficulty conceiving. Notably, **pain severity doesn't correlate well with disease extent**.

## Diagnosis and treatment

Suspected clinically, sometimes visible on specialist ultrasound or MRI, definitively confirmed by laparoscopy. Treatment: pain management, hormonal suppression, and surgical excision. There's no cure, but it is manageable.

**If your period pain regularly stops you functioning, this is worth raising explicitly — and worth pushing on if you're brushed off.**





## Adenomyosis

Endometrial tissue grows into the muscular wall of the uterus itself. Causes heavy, painful periods and an enlarged, tender uterus. Often coexists with endometriosis and is under-diagnosed. More common in people over **35** and after childbirth.





## PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome)

A common hormonal and metabolic condition affecting roughly **8–13%** of women of reproductive age, with a large share undiagnosed. Diagnosed by having **two of three**: irregular or absent ovulation, signs of excess androgens (acne, excess facial/body hair, scalp hair thinning), and polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound.

Often involves insulin resistance, and carries elevated long-term risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease — so it's a whole-body condition, not just a period problem. Management includes lifestyle measures, hormonal contraception for cycle regulation, metformin, and ovulation induction if trying to conceive.

Infrequent periods with PCOS means the endometrium can build up without regular shedding, which raises endometrial cancer risk over time — one reason it's actively managed rather than ignored.





## Fibroids (leiomyomas)

Benign muscular growths in or on the uterus. Extremely common — a majority of women develop them by age **50**, though many cause no symptoms. When symptomatic: heavy or prolonged bleeding, pelvic pressure, frequent urination, back pain. Treatments range from medication to targeted procedures (uterine artery embolisation, myomectomy) to hysterectomy.





## Heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia)

Blood loss over **80 ml**, or practically: soaking through protection hourly, needing double protection, passing large clots, bleeding over **7 days**, or waking at night to change. A leading cause of **iron-deficiency anaemia** — which presents as fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness, pale skin, brittle nails, and sometimes odd cravings for ice or non-food items (**pica**).

Causes include fibroids, adenomyosis, polyps, hormonal imbalance, bleeding disorders (**von Willebrand disease** is notably underdiagnosed in women), thyroid disease, and copper IUDs. Very treatable — tranexamic acid, NSAIDs, hormonal options, hormonal IUD, or procedures.





## Amenorrhoea

**Primary** — no period by age **15**, or by **13** with no other signs of puberty. **Secondary** — periods stop for **3+ months** in someone who previously had them.

## Causes

Pregnancy (always rule out first), breastfeeding, contraception, PCOS, thyroid disease, high prolactin, **functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea** (from low energy availability — undereating, over-exercising, significant stress, or low body weight), premature ovarian insufficiency, and structural causes.

## Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea

Deserves emphasis because it's often treated as a badge of athletic dedication when it's actually a signal the body has switched off reproduction to conserve energy — and it carries real consequences for bone density. It's a core component of **RED-S** (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport).





## Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)

Ovaries stop functioning normally before **40**, affecting around **1%**. Causes irregular or absent periods, hot flushes, and infertility. Important to identify because the resulting low oestrogen affects bone and cardiovascular health long-term, and hormone therapy is usually recommended until the typical age of menopause.





## Other conditions worth knowing

- **Endometrial and cervical polyps** — benign growths causing bleeding between periods
- **PID (pelvic inflammatory disease)** — infection of the reproductive organs; causes pain, unusual discharge, fever. Needs prompt antibiotics to prevent lasting damage
- **Thyroid disorders** — both under- and overactive thyroid disrupt cycles, and are easily tested for
- **Bleeding disorders** — worth considering if heavy periods have been the norm since menarche, especially with easy bruising or nosebleeds

# 14. The menstrual lifespan


*From first period through menopause — and why postmenopausal bleeding always needs checking.*

## Puberty and menarche

The first period usually arrives between **10 and 15** (average around **12**), typically about **2 to 2.5 years** after breast development begins and following a growth spurt and the appearance of vaginal discharge. Genetics, nutrition, body composition, and general health all influence timing.

**Cycles are commonly irregular for the first 1–2 years** because ovulation isn't yet consistent — cycles up to **45 days** are normal in that window. Practical preparation matters more than precision here: a small kit in a school bag, knowing where to get supplies, and knowing that the first period arriving unexpectedly is completely ordinary.

Worth medical attention: no period by **15**, no signs of puberty by **13**, or periods that are extremely heavy or disabling from the start.

## Reproductive years

Cycles typically settle into their most regular pattern in the 20s and 30s. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, contraception, weight change, and stress will all interrupt them, and cycles usually resume afterwards.

Fertility declines gradually from the early 30s and more steeply after **35** — but regular periods continuing doesn't guarantee fertility, and irregular ones don't rule it out.

## Perimenopause

The transition, often starting in the **mid-40s** (sometimes earlier) and lasting anywhere from a few months to over **10 years**. Oestrogen fluctuates erratically rather than simply declining, which is why symptoms can be so variable.

Signs: cycles shortening then lengthening, skipped periods, changes in flow (often heavier before becoming lighter), hot flushes and night sweats, sleep disruption, mood changes, brain fog, vaginal dryness, joint aches, and worsening PMS.

**Pregnancy is still possible during perimenopause**, so contraception remains relevant — generally until **12 months** after the last period if over **50**, or **24 months** if under **50**.

## Menopause

Defined retrospectively as **12 consecutive months with no period**. Average age is around **51** (roughly **45–55** is typical). Before **45** is early; before **40** is premature ovarian insufficiency.

**Any bleeding after menopause is not normal and needs prompt medical assessment** — it's usually a benign cause, but it's also the main early symptom of endometrial cancer, which is highly treatable when caught early. This is the single most important line in this section.

## Postmenopause

Symptoms often ease over time, though vaginal and urinary changes tend to persist and are very treatable with local oestrogen. Long-term, lower oestrogen means increased risk of osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease — which makes strength training, adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D, and blood pressure monitoring genuinely worth attending to. HRT is an option worth an informed discussion with a doctor; the risk picture is considerably more favourable than the widely-publicised early-2000s reporting suggested, particularly when started near the time of menopause.

# 15. When to see a doctor


*Practical thresholds — and how to make the appointment count.*

## Book an appointment if you have

- Bleeding through a pad or tampon every hour for several consecutive hours
- Periods lasting longer than **7 days**
- Clots consistently bigger than a **10p coin / quarter (~2.5 cm)**
- Pain that stops you attending work or school, or that painkillers don't touch
- Cycles consistently shorter than **21 days** or longer than **35**
- No period for **3+ months** (with pregnancy ruled out)
- Bleeding between periods, after sex, or after menopause
- Symptoms of anaemia: persistent fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness, pale skin
- Severe premenstrual mood symptoms affecting your relationships or work
- Pain during sex, urination, or bowel movements
- A significant, sustained change from your own normal
- No period by age **15**, or no signs of puberty by **13**
- Trouble conceiving after **12 months** of trying (or **6 months** if over **35**)

## Seek urgent care for

- Sudden severe pelvic pain, especially with fever, fainting, or vomiting
- Signs of toxic shock syndrome while using a tampon or cup: high fever, vomiting, rash, dizziness, confusion
- Very heavy bleeding with dizziness, a racing heart, or breathlessness
- A positive pregnancy test with severe one-sided pain and/or bleeding (possible ectopic pregnancy — a medical emergency)

## Making the appointment count

Bring **2–3 cycles** of tracked data — dates, flow, pain scores, symptoms. Be specific about *function* rather than describing pain in the abstract: "I miss one day of work every month" lands very differently from "my periods are painful." And if you feel dismissed, it is entirely reasonable to ask for a second opinion or a specialist referral. Delayed diagnosis in menstrual health is well documented, and self-advocacy is a legitimate and often necessary part of the process.

# 16. Myths worth retiring


*Common claims, corrected.*

**"You can't get pregnant on your period."** You can. Sperm survive up to **5 days**, and people with short cycles can ovulate soon after bleeding ends.

**"A 28-day cycle is the correct one."** It's an average. Anything from **21 to 35 days** is normal.

**"Bad period pain is just part of being a woman."** Some discomfort is expected. Pain that stops you functioning is a symptom, not a rite of passage, and normalising it is precisely why conditions like endometriosis take years to diagnose.

**"You lose a lot of blood."** Typically **30–80 ml** total. It looks like more because it's mixed with tissue and mucus and spread over days.

**"Periods sync between people living together."** The best-designed studies don't support this. Cycles of different lengths inevitably drift in and out of alignment by chance, which is what people notice.

**"You shouldn't exercise, swim, or bathe on your period."** All fine, and exercise generally helps cramps. Tampons, cups, and discs all work for swimming.

**"PMS is imaginary or an excuse."** The physiology — hormone withdrawal acting on GABA and serotonin systems — is well documented. PMDD is a recognised clinical diagnosis.

**"Missing a period means you're pregnant."** It's the first thing to rule out, but stress, weight change, illness, exercise, thyroid problems, and PCOS are all common causes.

**"Skipping periods on the pill is dangerous."** The pill bleed isn't a real period and isn't a physiological requirement. Worth confirming with your prescriber, but it's generally considered safe.

**"Menopause happens overnight at 50."** Perimenopause is a transition that commonly spans several years.

# 17. Glossary


**Adenomyosis** — Endometrial tissue growing into the uterine muscle wall.

**Amenorrhoea** — Absence of periods.

**Anovulation** — A cycle in which no egg is released.

**Basal body temperature (BBT)** — Resting temperature on waking; rises slightly after ovulation.

**Corpus luteum** — The structure a follicle becomes after releasing its egg; produces progesterone.

**Dysmenorrhoea** — Painful periods.

**Endometrium** — The uterine lining that thickens and sheds each cycle.

**Follicle** — A fluid-filled ovarian sac containing an immature egg.

**hCG** — The pregnancy hormone detected by pregnancy tests.

**HPO axis** — The hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian feedback loop controlling the cycle.

**Luteal phase** — Ovulation to the next period; usually 12–14 days.

**Menarche** — The first period.

**Menopause** — 12 consecutive months without a period.

**Menorrhagia** — Abnormally heavy menstrual bleeding.

**Mittelschmerz** — Mid-cycle ovulation pain ("middle pain").

**Myometrium** — The muscular wall of the uterus.

**Oligomenorrhoea** — Infrequent periods (cycles longer than 35 days).

**Perimenopause** — The transitional years before menopause.

**PMDD** — Premenstrual dysphoric disorder; severe premenstrual mood symptoms.

**PMS** — Premenstrual syndrome.

**POI** — Premature ovarian insufficiency; ovarian function ending before 40.

**Prostaglandins** — Compounds causing uterine contractions and cramping.

**TSS** — Toxic shock syndrome; a rare, serious bacterial toxin illness.
